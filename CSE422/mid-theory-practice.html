Drill: one GA iteration, course convention

0/1 knapsack from the notes: capacity 15 kg; items (weight, value) 1 (6, 30), 2 (3, 14), 3 (4, 16), 4 (2, 9). Population 1101, 1010, 0110, 1001. Do each step on paper, then open the answer.

Step 1 — fitness of each chromosome

1101 → 11 kg, 53 · 1010 → 10 kg, 46 · 0110 → 7 kg, 30 · 1001 → 8 kg, 39.

Step 2 — selection (best 3)

1101, 1010, 1001. The lowest (0110) is dropped.

Step 3 — crossover after bit 2: best with each of the other two

11|01 × 10|10 → 1110 and 1001.   11|01 × 10|01 → 1101 and 1001.

Step 4 — mutation: flip bit 4, bit 2, bit 1, bit 3 of the four offspring

1110 → 1111 (15 kg, 69) · 1001 → 1101 (53) · 1101 → 0101 (23) · 1001 → 1011 (12 kg, 55). New population: 1111, 1101, 0101, 1011. Best so far 1111 = 69, which is the optimum (all four items weigh exactly 15 kg).

Cram: definitions, formulas, traps

Flashcards

Fifteen traps that cost marks

  1. GBFS path cost is the sum of edge costs, never of h-values.
  2. A*: stop when the goal is popped, not when it is first generated.
  3. A*: f(m) uses h(m), and g must be updated when a cheaper route appears.
  4. Admissibility must hold at every node; one counter-example is enough for "no".
  5. Consistency is checked on edges, both directions in an undirected graph.
  6. Admissible does not imply consistent.
  7. Manhattan is inadmissible once diagonal moves are allowed.
  8. "Bigger h is better" only among admissible heuristics.
  9. Hill climbing also stops on equal neighbours, so a plateau stops it.
  10. SA always accepts a better move; T matters only for worse ones.
  11. SA is like hill climbing at low T and like a random walk at high T.
  12. In a GA, a minimisation problem needs an inverted fitness (1/cost).
  13. Never use single-point crossover on a permutation; use OX.
  14. Alpha–beta: MAX changes only α, MIN only β; children copy the parent's current values.
  15. State your choices: tie-breaking, encoding, crossover point, mutation type, OX fill order.
